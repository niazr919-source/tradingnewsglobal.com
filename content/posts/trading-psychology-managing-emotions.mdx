Two traders can follow the identical strategy and get opposite results. The difference is rarely analysis — it's **behaviour under pressure**. Trading psychology is the study of why we abandon good plans at exactly the wrong moment.

## The four emotions that cost money

### Fear

Fear shows up as closing winners far too early, hesitating on valid setups, or refusing to enter after a loss. It feels like caution but behaves like self-sabotage — you keep the small wins and miss the ones that pay for them.

### Greed

Greed is moving a target further away because a trade is going well, increasing size after a winning streak, or holding past your exit hoping for more. It converts winners into losers.

### FOMO (fear of missing out)

Chasing a move that has already happened, usually at the worst possible price. FOMO entries are typically unplanned, oversized, and made without a stop-loss.

### Revenge trading

The most destructive of all: trying to win back a loss immediately. Size increases, standards drop, and one manageable loss becomes a blown account.

> Revenge trading is not a strategy failure — it's an attempt to make the market apologise. The market cannot hear you.

## Why this happens

Loss hurts more than an equivalent gain feels good — a well-documented asymmetry in how people weigh outcomes. Under stress, the brain favours immediate relief over long-term consistency. Add leverage and a fast timeframe, and you have conditions engineered to provoke poor decisions.

Understanding this matters: you are not weak-willed, you are experiencing a predictable response. The solution is structural, not motivational.

## Practical defences

### 1. Decide everything in advance

Write the entry, stop-loss, target and position size **before** entering. A written plan made calmly outranks any decision made mid-trade.

### 2. Use hard limits

- Daily loss limit — when hit, close the platform
- Maximum trades per session
- Mandatory break after two consecutive losses

These work because they don't require willpower at the moment it's depleted.

### 3. Size so you can think clearly

If a position is large enough to make your heart race, it's too large. Anxiety destroys judgement far faster than a small position destroys an account.

### 4. Keep a journal with emotions included

Log what you felt, not just what you traded. Most traders discover that a handful of emotional trades caused the majority of their losses — a pattern that's obvious in review and invisible in the moment.

### 5. Judge process, not outcome

A well-executed trade that loses is a **good** trade. An impulsive trade that wins is a **bad** one that got lucky — and it teaches a lesson that will cost you later.

## A simple pre-trade checklist

1. Does this match my written setup?
2. Is my risk 1% or less?
3. Is my stop-loss placed before entry?
4. Am I calm, or reacting to the last trade?

If any answer is wrong, don't trade.

## The bottom line

You cannot eliminate emotion, but you can build a process that doesn't depend on being emotionless. Rules made in advance, small position sizes, and hard stopping points are what turn a strategy on paper into results in practice.
