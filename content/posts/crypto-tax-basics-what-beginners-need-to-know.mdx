In most countries, cryptocurrency is treated as **property or an asset**, not currency — which means using it can trigger taxes. This is one of the most overlooked parts of crypto, and getting it wrong can be expensive.

> **Important:** Tax rules vary significantly by country and change often. This is general education, not tax advice. Always consult a qualified tax professional for your situation.

## What is a taxable event?

A taxable event is any action that may create a tax obligation. In many jurisdictions, these commonly include:

- **Selling** crypto for fiat currency (e.g. dollars)
- **Trading** one crypto for another (e.g. BTC for ETH)
- **Spending** crypto on goods or services
- **Earning** crypto — from staking, mining, interest or as payment

And what typically is **not** a taxable event:

- **Buying** crypto with fiat and holding it
- **Moving** crypto between your own wallets
- **Holding** through price changes (unrealised gains)

## Capital gains: the core concept

When you dispose of crypto, the taxable amount is usually the difference between what you paid (your **cost basis**) and what you received.

| | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Bought 1 token for | $1,000 |
| Sold it later for | $1,600 |
| Taxable gain | $600 |

Many countries also distinguish between **short-term** and **long-term** holdings, often taxing assets held longer at a lower rate. The details differ everywhere — which is exactly why records matter.

## Why record-keeping is everything

The single biggest crypto tax mistake is poor records. Every trade, swap and reward needs a date, value and cost basis. This gets complicated quickly if you:

- Trade across multiple exchanges
- Use DeFi protocols
- Receive staking or airdrop rewards

Keep a running log from the start. Reconstructing years of activity later is painful and error-prone.

## Common mistakes to avoid

1. **Assuming crypto-to-crypto trades are tax-free** — in many places they aren't
2. **Forgetting earned crypto** — staking and rewards are often taxable as income
3. **Losing cost-basis records** when moving between platforms
4. **Ignoring small transactions** that add up across a year

## A sensible approach

- Track everything from day one, ideally with dedicated crypto-tax software
- Understand which events are taxable in **your** country
- Set aside a portion of gains for potential tax owed
- Consult a professional before filing, especially with DeFi activity

## The bottom line

Crypto taxes aren't optional, and "I didn't know" rarely helps with tax authorities. Understand what triggers a taxable event, keep meticulous records, and get professional advice for your jurisdiction. The effort is far cheaper than the alternative.
