Candlestick charts are the default view in almost every trading platform, and for good reason: each candle packs four pieces of information into one shape. Learning to read them is the foundation of technical analysis.

## Anatomy of a candle

Every candlestick represents one period of time — one minute, one hour, one day — and shows four prices:

- **Open** — price at the start of the period
- **Close** — price at the end
- **High** — the highest point reached
- **Low** — the lowest point reached

The **body** is the range between open and close. The thin lines above and below are the **wicks** (or shadows), showing the extremes that price reached but didn't hold.

Typically a candle is coloured green when the close is above the open, and red when it's below.

## What the shape tells you

| Shape | What it suggests |
| --- | --- |
| Long body | Strong, one-sided momentum |
| Small body | Indecision; buyers and sellers balanced |
| Long upper wick | Buyers pushed up but were rejected |
| Long lower wick | Sellers pushed down but were rejected |
| Almost no body | A pause or potential turning point |

> A wick is a record of rejection. It shows a price the market tried and refused to accept — which is often more informative than the close itself.

## Timeframes change the story

The same market looks completely different across timeframes. A dramatic reversal on a 5-minute chart may be an insignificant blip on the daily chart.

- **Higher timeframes** (daily, 4-hour): slower, more reliable, less noise
- **Lower timeframes** (5-minute, 1-minute): faster, noisier, more false signals

Many traders read the higher timeframe first to establish direction, then drop down for entry timing.

## A few common patterns

- **Doji** — open and close nearly equal; indecision
- **Hammer** — small body with a long lower wick after a decline; possible rejection of lower prices
- **Engulfing** — one candle's body completely covers the previous one; a potential momentum shift

## The honest caveat about patterns

Pattern names are useful shorthand, but they are **not** predictions. The same shape can mean very different things depending on where it appears.

Always ask:

1. **Where** is this happening — at a key level, or in the middle of nowhere?
2. **What came before** — a strong trend, or choppy sideways movement?
3. **Is there confirmation** — does the next candle support the idea?

A hammer at a well-tested support level after an extended decline is meaningful. The identical hammer in the middle of a range is noise.

## The bottom line

Candlesticks are a language for describing what buyers and sellers did during a period. Learn to read bodies and wicks as evidence of pressure and rejection, always consider context and timeframe, and treat patterns as clues rather than signals.
