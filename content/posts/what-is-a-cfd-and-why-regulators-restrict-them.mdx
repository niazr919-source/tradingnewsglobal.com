A contract for difference is an agreement with a broker to exchange the change in an asset's price between opening and closing a position. If the price rises and you were long, the broker pays you the difference. If it falls, you pay them.

You never own the asset. There is no share certificate, no delivery, no dividend entitlement in the ordinary sense. You own a contract whose value tracks something else.

CFDs are also the product sitting behind the retail loss statistics that regulators require firms to publish — a figure that has stayed between **70% and 80%** since disclosure became mandatory. Understanding the mechanism explains why.

## How the mechanism works

Suppose an asset trades at 100 and you open a long CFD on 1,000 units.

- **Notional value:** 100,000
- **Margin at 20:1:** 5,000
- Price rises to 105 → you gain 5,000, a 100% return on margin
- Price falls to 95 → you lose 5,000, your entire margin

The position size is 100,000. Your money at risk is 5,000. **Profit and loss are calculated on the full notional, not on your deposit** — which is the entire point of the product and the entire source of its danger.

## Why the product exists

CFDs genuinely do things that owning the asset does not:

- **Short easily.** Selling short in the underlying market can be restricted, expensive or operationally awkward. A short CFD is symmetrical with a long one.
- **Access many markets from one account.** Indices, commodities, currencies and shares in a single place.
- **Trade small sizes** of instruments whose real contracts are large.
- **Avoid some transaction taxes** in certain jurisdictions, though tax treatment varies and changes.

These are real advantages for short-term, hedged or professional use. They are much less relevant to someone who wants exposure to an asset over years.

## The three costs, and why they compound

This is where most of the damage happens, because the costs are individually small and continuous.

**1. The spread.** You buy at the ask, sell at the bid. Every position starts at a loss equal to the spread on the full notional.

**2. Commission**, where charged explicitly rather than being built into a wider spread.

**3. Overnight financing.** This is the one people miss. Holding a leveraged position overnight incurs a financing charge, because the broker is effectively funding the difference between your margin and the notional. It is **interest on the full position size**, charged nightly.

Consider what that means over time. A position with a notional of 100,000 financed at a few percent annually accrues charges every single night. Held for months, the financing alone can exceed any plausible gain, and it is deducted regardless of whether the trade works.

> CFDs are constructed for short holding periods. Every day you hold one, the arithmetic gets slightly worse.

## Why regulators intervened

The EU, UK and Australia each restricted retail CFD sales after reviewing firms' own client outcome data. The measures were similar in each case:

| Measure | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Leverage caps | Typically 30:1 on major FX pairs, lower on more volatile assets |
| Negative balance protection | You cannot lose more than your account balance |
| Margin close-out rule | Positions closed automatically at a set equity threshold |
| Mandatory risk warning | Firms must publish their retail loss percentage |
| Marketing restrictions | No bonuses or incentives to trade |

The United States takes a stricter line: CFDs are not permitted for retail clients at all.

Note what the regulators did **not** do. They did not ban CFDs for retail clients in most jurisdictions, and they did not claim the product is fraudulent. They concluded it is a legitimate instrument being sold with too much leverage to people who did not understand the cost structure.

## The negative balance point

This deserves separate emphasis because it is the difference between a bad trade and a debt.

Without negative balance protection, a gap through your stop can produce a loss larger than your account, and you owe the broker the difference. This has happened at scale during currency shocks, leaving retail clients with demands for sums far exceeding their deposits.

Negative balance protection is mandatory for retail clients in the EU, UK and Australia. It is frequently absent at offshore brokers advertising higher leverage. That single difference is the strongest practical argument for using a firm regulated where you live, and it outweighs any leverage advantage an offshore firm offers.

## Who CFDs actually suit

**They can make sense for** short-term directional positions, hedging an existing portfolio, or accessing a market that is otherwise impractical — used by someone who understands the financing cost and sizes positions accordingly.

**They are a poor fit for** long-term exposure to an asset. If you want to hold something for years, the financing drag makes a CFD strictly worse than owning the underlying or holding a fund that does.

The mismatch between what CFDs are built for and what many retail users want from them is a large part of the loss statistic. People use a short-duration leveraged instrument to express a long-duration unleveraged view, and pay financing for the privilege.

## Before you consider one

- Read the firm's **published retail loss percentage**. It is required to be on the site, and it is their own clients.
- Confirm the firm is **regulated in your jurisdiction**, on the regulator's register rather than the firm's site.
- Confirm **negative balance protection** applies to you.
- Calculate the **overnight financing** on your intended position and holding period before opening it.
- Use the **lowest leverage that makes the position worthwhile**, not the highest available.
- Size so that a gap beyond your stop is survivable, because stops do not guarantee a price.

## The bottom line

A CFD is a leveraged, financed bet on price movement in something you do not own. The leverage is the appeal and the financing is the tax, and both work against a position held for any length of time.

The published loss figures are not a warning label bolted on by regulators. They are the firms' own data about their own customers, and they have been remarkably stable across firms, countries and years.
