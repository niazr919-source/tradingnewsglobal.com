Leverage is the reason foreign exchange is accessible to people with modest capital, and it is the mechanism behind the large majority of destroyed retail accounts.

Both statements are true simultaneously, and the arithmetic explaining them takes about ten minutes to understand.

## What leverage is

Leverage lets you control a position larger than the capital you commit. The broker provides the difference; your capital acts as security.

At 30:1, depositing 1,000 units of currency lets you hold 30,000 of exposure.

The essential point, and the one that gets lost: **profit and loss are calculated on the full position, not on your deposit.** A 1% move on 30,000 is 300, which against 1,000 of capital is a 30% swing.

## Margin, in three terms

**Required margin** is the capital the broker holds against your open position. At 30:1 on a 30,000 position, that is 1,000.

**Free margin** is what remains available for new positions or to absorb losses.

**Equity** is your balance plus or minus unrealised profit and loss. This is the number that matters, and it moves continuously while positions are open.

## The liquidation table

This is the whole risk in one place.

| Leverage | Position on 1,000 | Adverse move that wipes margin |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1:1 | 1,000 | 100% |
| 5:1 | 5,000 | 20% |
| 10:1 | 10,000 | 10% |
| 30:1 | 30,000 | 3.3% |
| 100:1 | 100,000 | 1% |
| 500:1 | 500,000 | 0.2% |

Read the last column against how currencies actually behave. Major pairs commonly move more than 1% in a day. At 100:1 leverage, an ordinary day closes the account.

At 500:1 — still advertised by brokers outside regulated jurisdictions — a 0.2% move is sufficient. That is a routine fluctuation during any active session, which means the position is not a trade so much as a coin flip with a very short fuse.

## Margin call and stop-out

As equity falls, two thresholds matter.

**Margin call** is a warning that equity has dropped toward the required level. You can deposit more or close positions.

**Stop-out** is automatic liquidation once equity falls below the broker's threshold. Positions are closed for you, usually worst-loss first, at whatever price is available.

Treating the margin call as comfortable notice is a mistake, because in fast markets both thresholds can be crossed within the same move. During a gap, positions can be closed at prices well beyond the stop-out level.

**Negative balance protection**, required for retail clients in several jurisdictions, ensures you cannot lose more than you deposited. Where it is not required, you can end up owing the broker money. This has happened at scale during currency shocks, and it is one of the strongest arguments for using a regulated broker in your own jurisdiction.

## Why regulators intervened

The EU, UK and Australia each capped retail leverage after reviewing client outcome data, not on theoretical grounds. The caps are typically around 30:1 for major currency pairs, with lower limits for more volatile instruments.

The consistent finding was straightforward: higher leverage correlated with worse outcomes and faster depletion of accounts. Regulators also mandated negative balance protection and standardised risk warnings at the same time.

## Reframing the question

Most discussion asks what leverage to use. That is the wrong variable to set.

The right sequence is:

1. **Decide the risk per trade.** A common figure is 1% or less of account equity.
2. **Determine the stop distance** from your analysis, in pips.
3. **Calculate the position size** that makes those two consistent.
4. **Observe the leverage** that results.

Worked through: a 10,000 account, 1% risk, and a 50-pip stop. Risk allowance is 100. On a pair where a standard lot moves 10 per pip, 50 pips of movement on a standard lot is 500 — five times too much. The correct size is 0.2 lots, giving 2 per pip and a 100 loss at the stop.

The leverage employed falls out of that calculation. It is a consequence, never a target.

## Why the stop distance matters as much as the size

A tight stop permits a larger position for the same risk, which is why traders drift toward tight stops. The trade-off is that tighter stops are hit more often by ordinary noise.

There is no free choice here. Either you accept a wider stop and a smaller position, or a tighter stop and a higher probability of being stopped out of positions that would have worked. The one option unavailable is a tight stop, a large position, and a low stop-out rate.

## The costs leverage amplifies

Leverage magnifies more than the price move:

- **Spread** is paid on the full position size.
- **Overnight financing** accrues on the full notional, not on your margin, and compounds on positions held for weeks.
- **Slippage** is proportional to position size.

A leveraged position held for months can accumulate financing charges that exceed any reasonable expected gain, which is why leverage suits short holding periods and works against long ones.

## The bottom line

Leverage does not improve your analysis. It shortens the distance between being wrong and being removed from the position, and it does so on a scale most people do not check before opening a trade.

The single most useful habit is to look at the liquidation table above, find the row matching your leverage, and ask honestly whether the instrument you are trading routinely moves that far. For most retail traders using high leverage, the honest answer is yes — which is the finding buried inside every retail loss statistic.
